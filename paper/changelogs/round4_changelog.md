The capacity check (21) uses the flow rates and the demand on each link, which the simulator knows exactly (the demand is the larger of the summed flow rates and the observed arrivals). To test whether the benefit depends on this idealisation, the default controller was rerun on the unseen seeds with rate estimates carrying multiplicative log-normal noise (σ = 0.25, 0.5 and 1.0, mean-preserving, drawn afresh every tick), with a rate-blind check that assumes every active flow sends the mean active rate, and with telemetry-estimated rates, i.e. the mean number of packets each flow delivered per tick over the last five ticks (Table A1). The drop reduction does not shrink: guarded score reduces total drops by 5.06%, 4.81% and 4.36% with noisy rates, by 4.38% when rate-blind and by 5.72% with telemetry estimates, against 4.28% with exact rates, but its route changes rise from 34 to 44–103 per run. Delivered rates are lower than sending rates whenever packets are dropped, so telemetry estimates loosen the check; with them, the random risk signal removes 7.57% of the total drops, 1.85 points more than the score (95% CI of the difference [+0.46, +3.49]). A signal-free guard, in which every flow is eligible, candidates are plain shortest paths and the capacity check alone decides, reduces total drops by 3.49% with exact rates (0.79 points less than guarded score, 95% CI [−0.24, +1.87]) and by 6.80% with telemetry estimates, with 178 and 209 route changes per run. In this model, the capacity check therefore does not need exact per-flow rates and provides most of the drop reduction without any risk signal, and the signal mainly affects how many route changes are made. Real demand estimators may err in ways that independent noise does not capture. |

| 32 | Closed-loop flow transport and flow comp | (new paragraph) | Reordering penalty. The closed-loop runs were repeated with a conservative reordering penalty: every out-of-order delivery is treated by the sender as a loss signalled by duplicate acknowledgements, so half a round trip later the reordered packets are retransmitted spuriously (they consume capacity but add no progress) and the rate is halved, at most once per round trip together with real losses (Table A3). The guarded strategies are barely affected: their spurious retransmissions are 0.3% of the offered packets, and the mean FCT of guarded score stays 3.5% below that of ECMP (95% CI [−5.1%, −2.1%]; p99 4.4% below, [−8.0%, −0.4%]). Without admission rules, spurious retransmissions reach about 16% of the offered packets, and the mean FCT of the score-driven controller rises to 18.8% above ECMP ([+15.6%, +21.8%]; p99 +6.1%, [+1.7%, +14.2%]), while reactive and unguarded GNN rerouting reach 26.6% and 22.9% above ECMP. Because this penalty treats every reordered batch as a loss, whereas real transports tolerate some reordering (duplicate-ACK thresholds, time-based loss detection), the two models bracket the realistic case: the guard is clearly preferable when reordering costs as much as loss, and indistinguishable from the score-driven controller without rules when reordering is free. Which applies depends on the transport and cannot be settled in this model. |

| 53 | Load-balancing solutions relying on swit | (new paragraph) | FlowBender-style baseline. Since the required state exists in the simulator, a FlowBender-style baseline [20] was implemented: a link marks ECN when its queue exceeds 10% of its buffer, and a flow whose path contains a marking link for two consecutive ticks is rehashed to a randomly chosen different equal-cost path; there is no capacity check, budget or risk signal. On the benchmark seeds (Table V) it reduces total drops by 10.62%, 4.90 points more than guarded score (95% CI of the difference [+2.25, +8.09]), and has the lowest delivery delay of all strategies (mean 7.21 ms, 99th percentile 13.58 ms), but it makes 1,039 route changes per run and reorders 9.4% of the delivered packets in 42% of the flows; a more aggressive setting (5% threshold, one tick) gives 10.79% with 2,159 route changes. In the closed-loop model its mean FCT is 3.4% longer than that of guarded score without a reordering penalty ([+1.8%, +5.3%]) and 12.4% longer with it ([+9.8%, +14.5%]; Table A3). FlowBender-style rehashing therefore behaves like the score-driven controller without rules: it removes more drops and lowers open-loop delay than the guard, at the cost of much more churn and reordering, which lengthens flow completion in the closed-loop model. |
